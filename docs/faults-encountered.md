# The faults met while building this

Every row here is a **real** fault, one that actually happened, with its date.
None is hypothetical and none was written to make a point.

**What this file is for, and why it is not a diary.** A fault repaired by hand
stays in the head of whoever repaired it. Next time somebody else falls into
it — it happened on 29/08/2026 with the same orphan process, to two different
people, in the same night, and the second knew nothing of the first. This file
is the raw material of what Sailor has to learn to do by itself: read how it
went and derive a check from it. Until that loop closes, the list is kept by
hand.

The shape of every entry comes from a paper the research flow found in the
literature on 29/08/2026 — *Sillito & Kutomi, «Failures and Fixes: A Study of
Software System Incident Response»* — and from its conclusion, which here is
the rule: **what follows a fault is a check, not a task assigned to
somebody.** An entry without the «what would have prevented it» column is not
finished.

## What this table says, read all at once

**Almost half were seen only by running or by looking.** Not from the code, not
from the types, not from the tests: by timing something, by opening the store,
by taking a screenshot, by checking the weight of a directory. It is the reason
a test that could not have come out differently is not a test.

**Forty are still open** out of a hundred and fifty-four, and two of
those (10 and 28) are the same thing seen from two sides: **the same truth
written in several places, and no check that compares them** — two copies of a
list of components, and the reference resolution that lived in twelve places
before returning to one. It was a trio with 32 until a descriptor stopped being
able to declare a capability in one block and contradict it in the other.

**Numbers 50 and 51 were born elsewhere**, on a branch working in parallel while
the trunk took 41-47: they were a 41 and a 42, then a 48 and a 49, and now a 50
and a 51 — **renumbered twice in the same day**, because the second merge found
that the trunk had meanwhile taken those two as well. It is the third scar of
the same wound — 43 carries the first — and the cure is not to coordinate
better: it is that **a fault's number should be given by the system**, as the
count of open ones already is, instead of being chosen by whoever writes the
row.

*Twelve of those twenty-two say «closed in part», and for a while a middle state
took them out of the count: the check counted only the word «open», and the
prose said eleven. Nobody had decided that — 14 and 33 fell out of the count
without anyone noticing, and the direction of the error was the reassuring one.
Now a fault counts as open until the cure it declares is done, and the middle
state only tells which half is done.*

*Fault 39 was the first to leave the middle state, and it is worth saying how:
not by repairing better what was there, but by realising the missing half was
not inside it. The dry run was sound; it lacked a question, and the question is
put to the engine — never to the disk, which would have been the second copy of
the truth this table talks about in three different rows.*

*This row once named 4, 10 and 11 and said «the system does not know what it is
doing to itself». 4 and 11 were closed the same day — the store has a
`processes` table, and `crates/supervisor` keeps the window alive — so the
sentence was rewritten about whoever is open now. **A test watches the count;
which numbers it names, nothing does**: it went stale for a day and nothing
turned red.*

**31 and 32 say that the second copy does not check itself.** Both are the same
truth written in two places of the same file, with one of the two useless: `agy`
declares the chain and not the fallback, `gemini-cli` declares the capability
and not the command line. Neither breaks anything — that is the part to read. A
fallback that never fires looks like a fallback that was not needed; a
capability never used looks like a capability that is there. Fault 10 says the
same thing about code; these two say it about data, which is where the product
put the part it can change without recompiling.

**And both stay open for the same reason, which has to be said.** The cure for
each is a piece of data nobody has measured — how `agy` declares it has run out
of quota, how `gemini` declares the same — and the two could be written in
thirty seconds by guessing them. That would be worse than the defect: a wrong
word in `unusable_when` sends a malformed mandate down the whole chain until
somebody answers anyway, and no check would see it any more. **An open fault
with its missing measurement written beside it is worth more than a fault
closed on an invention.**

**Four new faults fought over two numbers, and it is fault 10 again.** 31, 32,
33, 34 and 35 were born the same day in four isolated trees, and three of those
trees wrote «31» and «32» without seeing each other. The isolation was there to
stop the code being overwritten, and it worked: but the numbering of this table
is a **shared, sequential** resource, and isolation does not protect it — it
makes it worse, because inside a tree nobody sees the others. The tests that
watch this file caught the duplicate at the first assembly, which is why they
exist. Whoever splits work across separate trees has to ask **which resources
stay common even in there**, and this is the first.

**Fault 11 says what a badly written cause costs.** The entry said «a compile
error kills the window», and anyone reading it would have tried to handle the
error better — where there was nothing to handle. The defect was the order of
two lines inside `tauri-cli`, and it shows only by reading the source of a tool
you trust. **An entry in this file is a diagnosis, and a wrong diagnosis sends
somebody to repair the wrong place**: here it cost the «what happened» line,
rewritten together with the repair.

**Fault 22 was already written down, and did not count as a fault.** The number
«total cost over the 16 runs: 0 on every one» sat in the `da-fare` note, in a
table of the store's state. Nobody read it as a defect until a decision had to
rest on it: a zero in a column of measurements looks like a system that has not
spent yet, not like a system that cannot count. **A missing figure disguises
itself as a calm figure**, and the only way to unmask it is to try to decide
something with it.

**Fault 24 is the first found by using the product rather than reading it.** Not
from reading the code, not from a test: from the first command that asked
Sailor something nobody had ever asked it — how much a run had cost. This
machine's store had been unreadable for a whole day and nobody knew, because
nobody read it. It is the strongest argument for using Sailor to develop
Sailor: **the tests cover the code, not the installation.**

**Not one was found by an automatic check.** All by a person looking. That is
the number that has to change — and 23 is the first that leaves behind a check
able to find it again: `cargo test -p sailor --test
identifiers_are_in_english` was born **red on 64 identifiers** and went green by
repairing them. It is not a test written afterwards to certify the work: it is
the measurement that said how much work there was.

**Three arrived in a single day, and all three by running.** 19 showed by
writing a file where the documentation said and watching nobody read it; 20 by
launching a flow with a wrong field; 21 by rerunning the battery twenty times in
a row instead of once. None of the three would have come out by reading the
code: two agreed with themselves, and the third showed only by repetition.

**Fault 15 says why.** Sailor had no commands to operate on itself, so whoever
worked on it went around it with external scripts — and a tool that is gone
around records nothing of what happens near it. It is the same defect as 4 and
10, at the root: **the system does not know what it is doing to itself because
it does not pass through itself.**

**Fault 26 says how long the chain is.** Three passes to find it: the author
wrote the test, the verifier mutated it — really, on a copy — and saw red, and
only a third who read it **without knowing whose work it was** noticed the
mutation was not the right one. The verifier had removed the cap; the real
defect was the fixed wait, and against that the test stayed green. «Whoever
makes a thing does not judge it» is not enough: **whoever judges must be judged
too**, and the way to do it is to take the author's name away from them.

**Fault 13 was already written down, and it did not help.** The zsh trap had
been in a memory for days: whoever rewrote the supervision — that is, whoever
had just finished repairing fault 12 — did not consult it and fell into it
twenty minutes later. It is the proof that a lesson written down is worth
nothing without the moment when somebody goes and reads it. For flows that
moment now exists, and it is `docs/decisions.md`, read at three points of the
development flow. For whoever writes by hand, it does not.
